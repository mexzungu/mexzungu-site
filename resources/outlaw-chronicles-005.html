Mission Briefing

Andy gave his first angel investor a board seat because it felt like the right thing to do. She had written the cheque, she knew people, and the conversation where he said no felt harder than the conversation where he said yes. By Series B, he needed three signatures to make a hire. This issue is about how that happens, and how to see it before it does.

 

Venture Odyssey

The board seat he gave away for free cost him his company

 

Departure: The night the cheque cleared

He said yes before the napkin finished sliding.

Andy is from Andromeda, and if you know anything about Andromeda, you know that people from there take obligations seriously. A cheque written is a cheque earned. When Ange wrote $75,000 on a napkin term sheet in a bar in the creative district and slid it across the table, Andy felt it the way you feel a handshake that means something. He said yes before the napkin finished sliding.

The term sheet was simple. $75,000. Convertible note. And one board seat.

"It's standard," Ange said. She wore a gut-instinct gauge pinned to her lapel like a badge of honour, and the gauge was pointing firmly at green. "Angels who write cheques at this stage want a seat. It's how I stay close enough to help."

Andy nodded. He had a co-founder, two advisors, and now a board of three: himself, his co-founder, and Ange. He controlled the board. He controlled the company. Ange was helpful, connected, and warm. She made introductions. She answered calls at midnight. She was, by every measure he had at the time, the right person in the room.

He got back to building. The company grew. He hired. He shipped. He raised a seed round eighteen months later, $600,000 from three angels on a SAFE, no additional board seats. He had been careful about that part, at least. The board stayed three: him, his co-founder, and Ange.

He remembers that period as the best one. Moving fast. Nobody in the way. The board meetings were brief because there were only three people in them and two of them worked at the company.

Layover: The seats that came with the money

Two of seven votes. He had not been counting. He had been building.

The Series A term sheet arrived when the company was doing $180,000 in monthly recurring revenue and growing at 15% month over month. The lead investor was a fund with a clean reputation and a partner who had built a company twice the size before she started writing cheques. The terms were reasonable. The valuation was fair.

Item 4 of the term sheet: Board composition. Five seats. Two founders. Two investors. One independent.

Andy read it quickly. Two founder seats, that was him and his co-founder. Two investor seats, that was the lead fund and one additional investor. One independent, to be agreed. He owned 59% of the company after conversion. He had two of the five votes. He was fine.

He signed.

Eighteen months later, the Series B term sheet arrived. The lead was a larger fund, later stage, portfolio of sixty companies. The terms were not unreasonable. The valuation was strong. Item 3: Board composition. Seven seats. Two founders. Three investors. Two independents.

Andy had two of seven votes. He owned 44% of the company.

His co-founder was technically a vote in his column, but his co-founder had been growing quieter for six months, and Andy had started to notice that the quietness had a shape to it.

The investor who held the third investor seat was Reti. Reti was from Zeta Reticuli, which is a binary star system, which means Reti had grown up watching two gravitational forces negotiate permanent equilibrium. This had given him a specific kind of patience. He smiled constantly. His briefcase contained term sheets with small red flags printed on them, which he had always found charming rather than alarming. He sat at the board table with his hands folded and his smile in place and his eyes tracking everything.

The first thing Reti's firm requested, three weeks after the Series B closed, was an executive hiring policy. Any hire above a certain salary level required board approval.

Andy read the policy draft and felt something settle in his stomach that he could not name. He called the lawyer who had done his Series A, a man in Barcelona who had been in enough of these rooms to know what the feeling was before Andy described it.

"He wants to run a hiring committee for every VP," Andy said.

"That is in the policy draft?"

"It is. Is that standard?"

There was a pause on the other end. Then: "It is a real provision. Whether it is standard depends on what you mean by standard. Companies that have accepted it exist. Companies that have not also exist. The question is whether you have the votes to reject it."

Andy counted. Two. He had two votes. Against five.

He had given Ange her board seat before he understood what a board seat was. She had voted with him on every material question in three years, which was easy when every material question was his idea and the company was going up and to the right. Now Reti's fund held two seats, the Series A fund held one, and the independent director had been proposed by the Series A fund and confirmed without objection eighteen months ago. Andy had not objected because he had not been counting. He had been building.

The hiring policy passed.

Four months later, the board declined to approve his VP of Engineering candidate. The stated reason was that the candidate lacked enterprise experience. Andy believed the real reason was that his candidate had worked for him before, at his last company, and Reti's fund preferred someone they already knew.

He hired someone else. The company performed fine. Andy never forgot the moment he understood he was no longer hiring the people he wanted.

He called the lawyer again that evening.

"When did I lose control of this?"

The lawyer was quiet for a moment. "Board control is not a single moment. It is a sequence of individually reasonable decisions that produce an unreasonable outcome. The first board seat went to Ange. That was reasonable at the time. The Series A added two investor seats. The Series B added a third. Each step was standard for the stage. The problem is that the sequence is always the same, and nobody shows founders the final frame before they agree to the first one."

Arrival: The frame nobody shows you first

Founders own the equity. The investors own the governance. These are not the same thing.

By the time a company reaches Series B, this is the typical board picture: two founder seats, three investor seats, one or two independents proposed by the investors. Founders own the equity. The investors own the governance. These are not the same thing, and the distance between them grows with every round.

Here is the mechanic. At pre-seed, an angel gets a board seat because the company needs credibility and the angel needs visibility. This feels like a trade. At Series A, the lead investor gets a board seat because a fund writing $3M needs representation. Their co-investor sometimes gets a second. At Series B, the new lead gets a seat. Sometimes two. The independent director was proposed at Series A and everyone has become accustomed to them by the time the Series B closes.

What a founder sees at each step is the seat being asked for, not the eventual composition.

It is like agreeing to let three people move into your house, one at a time, over three years. The first person is quiet and helpful and you barely notice. The second brings a friend. The third brings two friends. By year three, the house rules are being written by people who did not build the house and do not pay the mortgage.

Andy's story ends with him still at the company, still the CEO, still working. But he no longer makes unilateral decisions on anything that touches the board's purview, which, as the policies have accumulated, has come to include hiring above a certain level, any contract above a certain value, and any strategic pivot requiring capital reallocation. These are not unusual provisions. They are the standard architecture of late-stage governance. What is unusual is that nobody showed Andy the full picture in the bar when Ange was sliding the napkin across the table.

She was not trying to take his company. She wanted to help. The board seat was how angels helped, in her experience. The problem was not the intention. The problem was the sequence.

Check the sequence before you agree to the first seat.

 

The Blueprint

Five things to do before you grant a board seat

⚡ Map your board composition at each future round before you agree to the first board seat. Sketch the Series A board, the Series B board, the Series C board. If you cannot maintain a founder-friendly majority by Series B with the seats you are agreeing to now, negotiate differently now. Done looks like: a written board composition model showing founder vote count at each stage, signed off before any board seat is granted.
⚡ Negotiate protective provisions before they are requested. Founder protective provisions—the right to approve your own executive hires, veto on strategic pivots, right to remain as CEO absent cause—are easier to negotiate at pre-seed than at Series B. Done looks like: at least two founder protective provisions in your first shareholders agreement, reviewed by a startup lawyer who specialises in governance, not just documents.
⚡ Understand what your independent director vote actually means. Independent directors are often proposed by lead investors, nominated at board meetings where the investors have the votes to confirm them, and develop relationships with the fund over years of service on portfolio companies. "Independent" is a structural label. Done looks like: you have asked who proposed your independent, who has worked with them before, and you have your own candidate ready if you need to propose a counter.
⚡ Treat board composition as a negotiating point, not a courtesy. Every seat granted is a vote granted. Votes determine whether you keep your job, who you hire, and what you can do with the company you built. Done looks like: your next term sheet negotiation includes a written counter-proposal on board composition, reviewed by counsel before you respond.
⚡ Know the difference between information rights and board seats. Angels who want to stay close can have information rights: quarterly financials, cap table access, observer rights without a vote. A board observer sits in the room and says nothing that counts. Done looks like: your next angel conversation offers observer rights before you offer a seat, and you know which one you have agreed to before the term sheet is signed.

Curious Corner

Three things from the cosmos this week

⚡ The NVCA Model Voting Agreement. The National Venture Capital Association publishes the model investors' rights agreement and voting agreement used in most US venture deals. If you want to know what "standard" investor board rights actually look like before you negotiate, this is the document your investors are starting from.
⚡ Bessemer Venture Partners Anti-Portfolio. Bessemer's famous list of companies they passed on, including Google, Apple, and Facebook, with the actual reasoning at the time. Relevant here because governance and control were factors in several of those decisions. The smartest investors in the room still get the sequence wrong sometimes.
⚡ Sam Altman's Startup Playbook. YC's founding-era field guide to running a startup founder-led. Light on governance mechanics, which is exactly the point: the standard advice covers everything except the sequence this issue is about. Read it for what it says, and notice what it does not.
