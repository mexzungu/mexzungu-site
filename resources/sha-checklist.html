SHA Clause Checklist

Five clauses to check before the next time someone joins or leaves your cap table.

Most shareholders agreements look fine. The problem is not what is in them. It is the three things that are not. This checklist covers five provisions that are missing from most template agreements and only matter when something goes wrong. Check them today, before they check you.
01 Deadlock Resolution

Search your agreement for the word "deadlock." If it does not appear, or appears without a corresponding resolution process, your equal-split structure has no tiebreaker. Two founders with identical voting weight and no mechanism to break a tie cannot make binding decisions when it matters most.

Done looks like: A clause that says what happens if both founders disagree on a reserved decision and cannot resolve it in fourteen days. It must include at least one of: a casting vote assigned to a specific role, a mediation process with a named procedure, or a buy-sell mechanism.
02 Good Leaver vs. Bad Leaver

Find the leaver clause and write out in plain language what it actually says happens when a founder leaves. Then ask: does it distinguish between a founder who resigned, one who was pushed out, one who left for health reasons, and one who joined a competitor? If the outcome is the same for all four scenarios, the clause is not doing its job.

Done looks like: Your agreement uses the words "good leaver" and "bad leaver," defines both, prices the buyout differently for each, and names who makes the categorisation call.
03 Pre-existing IP Assignment

Identify every piece of IP the company relies on and trace it back to when it was created. Anything created before incorporation, or created by a founder using personal resources or pre-existing work, is not automatically company property even if your SHA says the company owns all its IP.

Done looks like: A signed IP assignment agreement, separate from the SHA, that formally transfers each piece of pre-existing IP to the company. If you incorporated through Stripe Atlas, Clerky, or similar, check whether this was included. It often is not.
04 Drag-Along Threshold

Find the drag-along clause and check the threshold. It should specify the percentage at which a majority of shareholders can require the rest to sell their shares in an exit. If that threshold is set at 50%, a minority shareholder can block a sale.

Done looks like: Your drag-along is set at a threshold that reflects how you actually want exit decisions made, and your lawyers have confirmed it works under the law of the jurisdiction where you are registered.
05 Thirty-Minute Legal Review

Book thirty minutes with a startup lawyer to review just these four points. Not to rewrite the agreement. Just to confirm whether the gaps exist and, if they do, what closing them would cost. Do this before the next time someone joins or leaves the cap table. Not after.

Done looks like: A written summary from the lawyer of what is missing and a quote for fixing it.
