The Wire

Tau took a 40% pay cut to join Vela's improbably terrestrial edtech concern. The offer was 2% equity, vesting over four years. He believed in both. Three years later, Series A lawyers found the grant had never been legally valid in the country where Tau lived. Nobody had noticed. That is the thing about page 12 of a legal review: it waits.

 

The Deep Dive

Your option plan is legal in Delaware. Nowhere else.

 

The Offer That Felt Like a Promise

The universe contains, at last count, an unreasonable number of legal jurisdictions. Scholars who have attempted to catalogue them estimate somewhere between four hundred thousand and several billion, depending on whether you count the dozen or so currently under appeal on grounds that they were established by civilisations that have since gone extinct. Most of these jurisdictions have nuanced, carefully considered rules about company formation, securities regulation, and the rights of equity holders. Almost none of them are Delaware.

Delaware is a small peninsula on the eastern seaboard of a minor planet in an unremarkable solar system. It has very good courts, a well-developed body of corporate precedent, and a surprisingly large proportion of the known universe's venture-backed startups. This is not entirely a coincidence. Delaware does not advertise. It does not need to. The word gets around.

Vela was from the Vela Pulsar, which is worth knowing because people from the Vela Pulsar tend to approach documentation the way astronomers approach data: meticulously, exhaustively, and in the sincere belief that if you have recorded everything correctly, the universe will behave accordingly. She had incorporated in Delaware. She had a 409A valuation. She had a standard equity incentive plan drafted by a firm in San Francisco. The boxes were checked. She moved on.

When she hired Tau, she sat across from him and told him he would own 2% of the company. Not "up to 2%." Not "a maximum of 2% subject to adjustments." Two percent. Vesting over four years, one-year cliff. Tau was from Tau Ceti, which orbits a star close enough in character to Earth's sun that people from Tau Ceti are generally considered adaptable. Tau had adapted. He was the kind of engineer who could have gone anywhere. The below-market salary only worked because the equity made the total package real. He signed. He started. He believed.

The San Francisco firm had done exactly what they were asked. They drafted an option plan for a Delaware company, gave it board approval, a 409A peg, standard ISO and NSO mechanics for US employees, proper exercise windows. It was a clean plan. It was designed for US employees. Tau lived in Kenya, and nobody had asked.

What Nobody Checked

For three years, Tau received his grant letters, his vesting schedules, his annual statements. He watched the cap table on paper. He watched the company grow. He told his family he would own 2% of something that was going somewhere.

Here is what was happening in the background, in language that the grant letters did not contain.

Stock options issued by a Delaware corporation to an employee in Kenya are not, legally speaking, a domestic transaction. They are a cross-border securities offer. The Capital Markets Authority in Kenya regulates what constitutes a security, how securities can be offered to Kenyan residents, and what registration or exemption requirements apply before that offer is valid. An option grant from a foreign company to a Kenyan employee can constitute an offer of a security under Kenyan law. If the company has not complied with those requirements before issuing the grant, the grant may not be enforceable in the jurisdiction where the employee would actually need to enforce it.

Think of it this way. A driving licence issued in one country gives you full legal authority to drive there. Cross an international border, and that authority does not automatically follow you. Whether it is valid in the next country depends on that country's rules, bilateral agreements, and registration requirements. Your licence is real. The recognition of it is a separate question entirely. The option grant worked the same way. It was issued in Delaware. Tau lived somewhere else. Nobody had asked whether the grant was recognised where he lived.

I have been in that room. The room where page 12 of a legal review surfaces exactly this problem. I remember the exact quality of the silence when a founder reads it for the first time. It does not look like a crisis from the outside. It looks like a checkbox. For the employees whose grants are at risk, it is not a checkbox.

The Page Nobody Reads Until the Deal

Vela's Series A diligence arrived on a Tuesday. The investor's legal review was methodical, thorough, and fair. On page 12, it said, in straightforward language, that the company had not obtained legal opinions on the enforceability of option grants in the jurisdictions of residence of non-US employees.

The call with her lawyers was short. She asked one question.

"What does that mean for Tau?"

The answer came back after a pause that lasted slightly too long. "It means we need to fix the structure. We'll create a new grant under a compliant framework. His vesting will reset from the date of the new grant."

She said nothing for a moment. Then: "So he loses three years."

Not entirely. But the new grant was structured differently, with a different number, and it referenced a company now worth considerably more than it had been when Tau started. The effective value of what he received was significantly less than the 2% of the company he had believed he was holding.

He stayed through the close. He left six months after.

The original plan had been legal. Delaware had nothing to do with it.

Here is the part that repeats. Not because founders are reckless, and not because lawyers are careless. Because the incentive structure of startup formation points everyone toward the US framework, the Delaware entity, the standard plan, and the San Francisco firm. The ecosystem is optimised for that path. The non-US employee who joins early and takes a pay cut to do it is a detail that the standard template was not built to accommodate.

ISOs, the preferred option type in US plans, apply only to US employees. NSOs can be issued internationally, but the tax treatment in the employee's country of residence governs how and when that employee is taxed. In some jurisdictions, the taxable event is at exercise, before any cash has been received. In others, there are registration requirements that must be satisfied before the grant is issued. In a few, there are restrictions on foreign companies offering equity to local residents without specific exemptions. None of this is visible from the plan document. The plan document does not know where the employee lives.

The exercise price compounds this. A standard plan pegs the exercise price to the 409A valuation. When a non-US employee exercises, the tax event, the currency conversion, and the local regulatory treatment all stack on top of that price. An employee in a jurisdiction with aggressive withholding may face a tax liability on paper gains before they have received any cash. If they cannot afford to exercise without selling shares they cannot yet sell, the option is worth nothing to them in practice, regardless of what the grant letter says.

The fix is not complicated. It requires asking the question before the grant is issued, not after the investor's lawyers surface it on page 12.

 

The Blueprint

Jurisdiction Audit: 5 Steps

⚡ List every country where your option holders actually live. Not where they are employed on paper. Where they pay taxes and would need to enforce their grant. Run this list today. If you have employees across more than one jurisdiction, the list should take you fifteen minutes. Done looks like: a spreadsheet with one row per non-US option holder, their country of tax residence, and a column currently reading "unknown" next to local enforceability. That column is your work order.
⚡ Commission a short legal memo for each jurisdiction on the list. Two to three hours of local counsel's time, per country, to confirm whether your current plan is enforceable there. Not your US plan counsel. A lawyer admitted in that country. The memo should answer three questions: does the grant constitute an offer of securities under local law, what registration or exemption applies, and what is the tax event for the employee. Done looks like: a signed memo from local counsel for each jurisdiction, filed with the equity plan documents. This is not expensive. It is almost always cheaper than fixing the problem at Series A diligence.
⚡ Add international compliance schedules before you issue the next grant. Carta, Pulley, and Ledgy all offer jurisdiction-specific compliance schedules that modify grant terms to match local requirements. These are not workarounds. They are built exactly for this problem. If you have non-US employees, look for the international equity compliance section before you click issue. Done looks like: every grant to a non-US employee is issued under a jurisdiction-specific schedule, not the default plan template.
⚡ Confirm your non-US employees can actually afford to exercise. Model the exercise for each non-US option holder at your current 409A price, plus the estimated local tax liability at exercise, in their local currency. If the total cost exceeds what a reasonable employee earning their salary could pay from savings, the option is not functioning as retention equity. It is theoretical. Done looks like: for each non-US holder, you know the exercise cost, the tax liability, and whether a cashless exercise or net settlement structure is available and appropriate.
⚡ Run a 409A valuation check. If your last 409A was more than twelve months ago, or before a material funding event, your option grants since then have pricing compliance risk. A stale 409A is a separate problem from the jurisdiction issue, but it layers on top of it. Both are surfaced in Series A diligence. Done looks like: a current 409A from a qualified provider, dated after your last material event, on file with your option grants.
