The Mexzungu Proposal Structure

Apr 2026 Business 6 sources 10 min read

Key Findings

  • Buyers buy when they feel understood, not when they feel sold to. The entire proposal structure should serve this single insight.
  • The "What Breaks If You Don't Act" section is missing from all current Mexzungu proposals and is the highest-leverage addition. SPIN Selling data: surfacing implications before solutions doubles close rates.
  • McKinsey, Goldman Sachs, and top-tier law firms all lead with the client's situation, not their credentials. No "Why Us" section exists in their standard structures.
  • Prospect Theory (Kahneman): loss framing is 2x more motivating than equivalent gain framing. "This structure will prevent a problem" is half as motivating as naming the specific thing that breaks without action.
  • The CTA must have exactly one primary action. Hick's Law and the Paradox of Choice both confirm: two primary actions effectively produce zero conversions.

The Central Insight

Buyers buy when they feel understood. Not when they feel sold to.

Every element of a proposal either increases the feeling of being understood or introduces the feeling of being sold to. The moment the document shifts from "this person gets my situation" to "this person is trying to close me," the buyer's defenses activate and the decision slows or stops.

This is not intuition. It is the convergent finding of four decades of B2B sales research, behavioral economics, and buyer psychology. The structural implication: a proposal should be built from the buyer's emotional perspective, not the seller's logical one.

The seller's instinct: introduce, credential, propose, price, close. The buyer's psychology: recognize, disturb, solve, confirm, act. These are different sequences. The gap between them is where most proposals fail.

The Buyer's 8-Moment Journey

Every structural decision in the proposal should serve one of these eight moments.

Moment 1

Arrival (Hero section)

State: Skeptical, scanning. Question: "Is this actually about me, or is this a template?"

What they need: To see their situation in the first sentence. Precise, not broad.

What kills it: Any sentence about the firm before the client's situation is named.

Moment 2

Recognition (What we heard)

State: Defenses lowering, leaning forward. Question: "Wait. They actually listened."

What they need: The diagnosis to go deeper than what they said out loud. Name the thing they were thinking but did not say.

What kills it: Generic problem statements. Buzzwords. Anything they could have written themselves.

Moment 3: Highest Leverage

Disturbance (What breaks if you don't act)

State: Constructive urgency. Question: "I hadn't thought about it that way. How bad is this actually?"

What they need: Quantified stakes. Specific consequences. The cost of inaction made concrete, not vague.

Note: This is the highest-leverage moment in the entire document. If this section lands, pricing feels obvious. If it doesn't, pricing feels expensive. Currently missing from all Mexzungu proposals.

What kills it: Fearmongering without evidence. Vagueness. Anything they already knew.

Moment 4

Clarity (What we build)

State: Forward-looking, constructive. Question: "Can I picture what this produces?"

What they need: Specific deliverables that map directly to the risks named in Moment 3. Not service descriptions. Outputs.

What kills it: Generic workstream language. Any deliverable that doesn't connect to a named risk.

Moment 5

Decision Anxiety (What this costs)

State: Peak friction. Highest-stakes moment in the document. Question: "Is this worth it? Can I justify this?"

What they need: One clear recommended path. Anchor high. Milestone payments that break the commitment into small yeses.

What kills it: Options grid without a recommendation. Price presented before the problem has been made fully real.

Moment 6

Permission-Seeking (Track record)

State: Tentative yes, looking for one more confirmation. Question: "But can they actually do this?"

What they need: One or two specific parallel cases. Not generic credentials. A link for self-directed exploration.

What kills it: Too much here. They have already decided. Generic testimonials. Logos without context.

Moment 7

Landmine Scan (Fine print)

State: Protective, reading fast. Question: "What am I agreeing to? Is there a trap?"

What they need: Brevity. Confidence. Four lines maximum. Short fine print signals: "We are not trying to trap you."

What kills it: Long clauses. Legalese. Anything unexpected at this stage.

Moment 8

Act (CTA)

State: Ready (if above worked) or stalling (if it didn't). Question: "What do I do right now?"

What they need: One primary action. One safety valve. Nothing else.

What kills it: Multiple options. Weak language. Anything that creates a new decision.

The Recommended Structure

HEROOne headline that names their situation, not ours

Recipient name, company, valid date. One line that activates reciprocity: "You came to the call. We listened." No firm introduction. No positioning statement. Test: cover the logo. Does the headline still obviously describe this specific client?

01What We Heard

Mirror the diagnosis call with precision. Name the thing they said. Then name the thing they were thinking but didn't say. Do NOT propose anything here. Just reflect. The more accurate the reflection, the less selling you have to do. Test: could the client have written this section themselves? If yes: go deeper.

02What Breaks If You Don't Act HIGHEST LEVERAGE

Name the specific consequences of inaction. Quantify wherever possible. Name the window: 60 days, 90 days, the next VC meeting, the term sheet that's coming. Done right, this section makes the pricing in section 04 feel obvious. Done wrong or skipped, pricing feels like a debate.

03What We Build

Present the solution as the specific answer to the specific risks named in section 02. Each workstream maps to a named risk. Each deliverable is a concrete output, not a service description. "We build X so that Y cannot happen at DD" not "We provide advisory services for Z." Test: does each deliverable connect to a specific risk from section 02?

04What This Costs

Anchor high. Show the full engagement value first. Highlight one recommended path. Present milestone payments. Each one is a small yes that makes the big yes easier. Test: is there one obviously recommended option? If the client has to decide between equals, redesign.

05Track Record

2-3 sentences maximum. Specific parallel cases. "We built this exact dual-entity structure at Duara. They are now scaling from 4 to 40 schools." Not: "We have 20 years of combined experience." One link to /work. Nothing else.

06Fine Print

Four lines. No more. Mauritius law. 30-day termination. Confidential. This is consulting, not legal advice. Brevity is the message. Test: is it under 60 words? If not, cut.

CTAOne primary action, one secondary

Primary: Review and sign the engagement letter. Secondary: Book 30 minutes if not ready. Active language. Not "Please feel free to reach out." Yes: "Ready? Sign here. Not ready? Book a call."

Evidence by Section

Why the Hero Must Name Their Situation

Buyers decide within the first 7 seconds of reading whether a document is for them or generic (Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking research). The first sentence is the highest-leverage word in the document. Any sentence that names the firm before the client's situation fails the test.

Why Disturbance Is the Highest-Leverage Section

SPIN Selling (35,000 sales calls, 12 years) identified implication questions as the single highest correlate with close rates in complex B2B deals. In a written proposal, the disturbance section IS the implication question. Rackham's data: sellers who surfaced strong implications before presenting solutions closed at 2-3x the rate of those who moved directly from problem to solution.

Prospect Theory (Kahneman and Tversky) adds the mechanism: loss aversion is approximately 2x more motivating than equivalent gain. "This structure will prevent a cap table problem" (gain) is half as motivating as "an unformalized 15% stake is a material DD red flag that a VC will use as a negotiating lever against you at term sheet" (loss).

Why Track Record Comes After Pricing

Social proof is most effective at the point of maximum uncertainty (Cialdini). For a buyer reading a proposal, maximum uncertainty occurs at the pricing section: "is this worth it?" Placing credentials before pricing answers a question the buyer is not asking yet. Nielsen Norman Group A/B testing on B2B services pages confirms: social proof after pricing increases conversion. Social proof before pricing increases time-on-page with no conversion lift.

Why "Why Us" Is Specifically Harmful

The StoryBrand framework identifies the most common structural mistake in B2B marketing: the firm makes itself the hero. A standalone "Why Us" section is the most direct way to trigger this error. At the moment the buyer is in the Recognition phase (defenses down, feeling understood), a credential block pulls them into Evaluation mode. This is a state-change in exactly the wrong direction.

What the Best in the World Do

McKinsey: Never opens with credentials. Opens with "the situation," a precise framing of the client's strategic context that demonstrates understanding beyond what the client disclosed. Credentials live in the appendix. No "Why McKinsey" section exists in their standard proposal structure.

Goldman Sachs M&A advisory: Leads with "why now." The bank does not appear in the first three pages. The situation creates urgency before the solution is proposed. Credentials are assumed, not argued.

Freshfields, Linklaters: No "Why Us" sections. The depth of the problem analysis IS the demonstration of competence. A shallow analysis followed by a credential block is weaker than a deep analysis with no credentials at all.

The pattern across all three: the strongest sellers in the world lead with understanding, not identity. They make the buyer feel the problem before they introduce the solution.

What Never Appears in a Mexzungu Proposal

  • Any sentence about the firm before the client's situation is named
  • "Why Us" as a standalone section
  • Generic credentials ("20 years combined experience")
  • Pricing without a clear recommended option
  • A CTA with more than two actions
  • Fine print over 60 words
  • A deliverable not connected to a named risk
